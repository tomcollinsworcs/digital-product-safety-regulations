A. Primary Policy and Scope

1. Aims

1.1 The primary aims of these Regulations are to:

  1. Ensure the safety of digital products supplied in the UK

  2. Align regulation of digital products with existing physical product safety regulations

  3. Place practical responsibility for safety onto providers and enhance enforcement

  4. Protect consumers both individually and collectively (societally)

  5. Manage hazards presented through both exposure and interaction

  6. Define obligations for both generative and transmissive tools and services

1.2. Secondarily, they seek to:

  1. Provide a level playing field for commercial competition

  2. Establish a technical standards ecosystem for digital product safety

  3. Provide the foundations for a market for digital safety tools and products

2. Definitions

2.1 Product

A digital Product is any software or digital content that is sold, licensed or made available for use in the UK, in the course of a commercial activity, whether for payment or free of charge, that is directly operable or capable of being sensed by a human user.

Any software that has a human interface, or which directly determines the content or behaviour of a human interface is a digital Product.

Any website, app, program, or software-generated service that allows a user to directly interact with software without a human intermediary is also a digital Product.

2.2 User

Individual users include the intended consumers, or anyone reasonably likely to use the product.

Collective users are understood at a societal level and include people who could reasonably expect to be impacted by the product even if they do not directly use it themselves.

2.3 Provider

Any Developer, Operator or Distributor of a digital Product is a Provider

B. Essential Requirements

The essential requirements laid down in this Regulation are compulsory.

The essential requirements are to be interpreted and applied in such a way as to take into account the state of the art at the time of development and deployment as well as technical and economic considerations which are consistent with a high degree of health and safety protection.

1. General Requirements

1.1. Safety in Normal Use

Digital products shall be so defined, designed and developed as to operate safely and present no danger to individual persons, groups or society, when normally used.

1.2. Risk Assessment

The developer is under an obligation to analyse and assess the risks in order to identify those which apply to their product. They shall then design, develop and operate it taking into account its risk assessment.

1.3. Safety by Design

In selecting the most appropriate solutions, the developer shall apply the principles set out below, in the following order:

  1. eliminate or reduce risks as far as possible (inherently safe design and operation);

  2. take the necessary protection measures in relation to risks that cannot be eliminated;

  3. inform users of the residual risks due to any shortcomings of the protection measures adopted and indicate whether any particular precautions are required.

1.4. Foreseeable Misuse

When defining, designing and developing the digital product, the provider shall envisage not only the intended use of the product, but also the reasonably foreseeable uses.

2. Safe Design Requirements

2.1. Informational Hazards

The provider must identify, evaluate and control hazards arising from the use of a product in thecreation, selection, ranking, sequencing, synthesis, omission, framing, salience or presentation ofinformation.

Specifically, they must assess hazards arising from:

  1. Epistemic distortion
    for example; confabulation; fabricated evidence; misleading medical or scientific claims; overconfident AI output;

  2. Misleading framing and selective contextualisation
    for example; selective framing; decontextualisation; one-sided presentation;

  3. Synthetic or inauthentic signal production
    for example; deepfake; fake accounts; synthetic impersonation; coordinated inauthentic behaviour;

  4. Affect-skewed production or selection bias
    for example; negatively saturated framing; fear saturation; outrage-driven engagement; despair narrative;

  5. Traumatic or graphic content exposure
    for example; traumatic imagery; unwarned graphic content; realistic depictions of violence or death;

  6. Self-destructive ideation framing
    for example; suicide contagion framing (Werther effect); romanticised portrayal; absence of help-seeking framing;

  7. Harm-enabling instructional content
    for example; criminal instruction; extremist operational guidance; weapons-related technical detail;

  8. Output-distribution and prevalence distortion
    for example; filter bubble; agenda-setting; radicalising recommendation pathway; prevalence inflation;

  9. Source-authority degradation
    for example; source laundering; expertise-denial; unjournalistic news; false equivalence;

  10. Deceptive capability or product representation
    for example; unrealistic product presentation; overstated capability claims; misrepresented accuracy;

  11. Dehumanising or discriminatory content patterns
    for example; dehumanisation; othering; coded discriminatory signalling; success-signalling of harmful conduct;

  12. Sexualised-script normalisation
    for example; unrealistic relationship or performance expectations; algorithmic escalation toward extreme content; transactional-sexuality scripting;

  13. Cognitive overload via volume or contradiction
    for example; high-volume low-quality content; contradictory content flooding; undifferentiated AI-generated volume;

  14. Self-referential exposure narrowing
    for example; body-image loop; grievance reinforcement; rumination loop;

  15. Adversarial intergroup framing
    for example; scapegoating; civilisational threat narrative; zero-sum identity framing;

  16. Worth-signalling salience and comparison-inviting presentation
    for example; vanity metrics; popularity-as-worth cue; public ranking display;

  17. Uncapped directed propagation and convergence
    for example; coordinated harassment; brigading; mass reporting.

2.2. Agency and Autonomy Hazards

The provider must identify, evaluate and control hazards arising from systems that impair informed choice, consent, self-determination, self-regulation, resistance, exit or reflective control, for example through reinforcement, conditioning, habit formation, behavioural optimisation or reward/punishment structures, as well as hazards emerging from the unsafe capture, retention, manipulation or overload of attention.

Specifically, they must assess hazards arising from:

  1. Behavioural-compulsion reinforcement loop
    for example; variable-ratio reinforcement; loot box mechanics; near-miss effect; streaks;

  2. Continuous boundary-less delivery
    for example; infinite scroll; autoplay chain; continuous-refresh feed;

  3. Interruption and involuntary re-engagement
    for example; notification stacking; simultaneous feeds; return-pressure loop; urgency alert;

  4. Manipulative choice-architecture
    for example; coercive default; deceptive consent flow; cancellation friction;

  5. Emotional-pressure interaction design
    for example; confirmshaming; guilt prompts; flattery; gaslighting; simulated obligation;

  6. Non-critical validation and sycophantic calibration
    for example; sycophancy; uncritical affirmation; confirmation without challenge; unqualified reassurance in sensitive contexts;

  7. De-skilling via decision or judgment substitution
    for example; automation complacency; cognitive offloading; judgment substitution;

  8. Vulnerability and state-targeting
    for example; emotional-state targeting; crisis-state targeting; biometric targeting;

  9. Commercial pressure mechanics
    for example; fake scarcity; fake urgency; fake social proof;

  10. Commercial misdirection
    for example; disguised ads; hidden costs; price comparison prevention;

  11. Commercial induction via deliberate under-provision
    for example; underpowered free tier; bait and switch; restricted-functionality teaser;

  12. Data and memory exploitation
    for example; default data-sharing; disguised data collection; safety blackmail; contact list harvesting;

  13. Escapist or immersive substitution loop
    for example; immersive escapism; extreme first-person immersion; compulsive fantasy substitution;

  14. Relational-bond leverage over autonomy
    for example; guilt-on-leaving prompt; bond-contingent retention pressure; escalating obligation pressure;

  15. Divided-allegiance agent
    for example; hidden principal; covert steering; undisclosed allegiance.

2.3. Social-Relational Hazards

The provider must identify, evaluate and control hazards arising from mediation of interpersonal status, belonging, identity, intimacy and reputation.

Specifically, they must assess hazards arising from:

  1. Anthropomorphic or relational bonding formation
    for example; anthropomorphism; parasocial bonding; pseudo-intimacy; endearment-cue design; playacting;

  2. Social-cohesion and cross-cutting-tie degradation
    for example; cyberbalkanization; fragmented public sphere; tie dissolution;

  3. Relational or companionship dependency structuring
    for example; AI companion dependency; reliance on synthetic advice; emotional validation dependency;

  4. Human-relationship displacement
    for example; relational displacement; isolation reinforcement; platform-mediated validation;

  5. Common-knowledge and false-consensus distortion
    for example; false consensus effect; fragmented narrative environment; astroturfing; non-human opinion injection;

  6. Societal attitude-norm distortion
    for example; prejudice normalisation; attitude-norm drift; dehumanisation at scale;

  7. Status-as-quantified-worth norm distortion
    for example; popularity-as-worth norm; prestige allocation distortion; beauty/status competition culture;

  8. Relational-norm distortion
    for example; relationship-norm reset; synthetic-to-human expectation transfer;

  9. Sexual-cultural norm distortion
    for example; consent-norm erosion; gendered-script distortion; script normalisation at scale;

  10. Relational-capacity asymmetry
    for example; non-human responsiveness norm; always-on companion expectation; unmeetable-baseline effect;

  11. Undisclosed automation in identity-bearing interactions
    for example; bot-authored content presented as human; human-agent indistinguishability; undisclosed automation.

2.4. Institutional and Systemic Hazards

The provider must identify, evaluate and control hazards arising both directly and indirectly from incorporation of the product into wider systems, with consideration of platform governance, organisational incentives, infrastructure dependency, opacity, scale, market structure or system-level feedback.

Specifically, they must assess hazards arising from:

  1. Accountability and audit-trail failure
    for example; weak audit trail; non-reporting of failure; masking of harm;

  2. Correction-loop failure and normalisation of deviance
    for example; normalisation of deviance; correction lag; broken feedback loop;

  3. Opaque governance
    for example; hidden moderation; selective visibility restriction; inaccessible appeal;

  4. Misaligned organisational incentive structures
    for example; impression management; patronage; commercial optimisation against safety;

  5. Network-coupling cascade vulnerability
    for example; viral cascade; phantom signals; tight coupling;

  6. Centralisation and single-point-of-failure dependency
    for example; single point of failure; vendor lock-in; infrastructure concentration;

  7. Market and institutional distortion
    for example; flash crash; procedural subversion; automated socially-sensitive decisioning;

  8. Democratic-process degradation
    for example; candidate deepfake; false electoral momentum; bandwagon effect;

  9. Human-function displacement without accountability transfer
    for example; accountability gap; removal of human discretion; replacement of human judgement.

3. Platform Policing Requirements

For products with transmissive capabilities (i.e. communication tools), the developer must identify and implement appropriate measures for prevention, intervention and facilitation of formal policing.

  1. Passive prevention

    Products shall incorporate affordances that reduce the probability of rule-breaking before it occurs,
    for example; constraint (quantitative or logical limits), signalling (rules made salient), choice architecture, friction (increasing cost of non-compliance)

  2. Active Prevention

    Products shall incorporate reactive affordances that modify observed behaviour,
    for example; conditional rules, feedback loops, system-wide optimisation, content filters, behavioural filters

  3. Detection and Attribution

    Products shall incorporate systems that identify violations and link them to actors,
    for example; sensing, recording, identification, logging

  4. Intervention and enforcement

    Operators shall implement and maintain capacity to act once a violation is detected or suspected,
    for example; interception, sanction, removal or restriction, escalation to human authority

  5. Enablement of formal policing

    Developers and operators shall implement and maintain infrastructure that allows human policing to operate effectively,
    for example; access points, operational spaces, legal exemptions, interoperability

4. State of the Art Requirements

State of the art refers to the level of safety capability reasonably achievable using commercially available and sufficiently validated technical, organisational and design measures, taking account of current scientific knowledge, standards, incident data, and the characteristics and vulnerabilities of users.

4.1 Duty to Incorporate State of the Art

A provider must ensure that products incorporate state-of-the-art safety measures where necessary to reduce risk to an acceptable level.

4.1.1 This duty applies:

  1. at design and development;

  2. prior to placement on the market;

  3. throughout the product lifecycle.

4.1.2 Providers must conduct and document a State of the Art Review:

  1. prior to market placement;

  2. at prescribed intervals;

  3. in response to system changes or regulatory developments;

  4. following serious incidents or emergence of new safety information.

4.1.3 The review must consider:

  1. newly available safety measures;

  2. relevance to identified risks;

  3. feasibility and compatibility;

  4. proportionality of implementation.

4.2 Duty to Update

4.2.1 Where a material improvement in safety becomes commercially available:

  1. providers must incorporate it into future production within a specified period;

  2. where necessary to address serious risk, they must take corrective action for products already in use.

4.2.2 Corrective action may include:

  1. software updates,

  2. retrofit,

  3. safety notices,

  4. withdrawal or recall.

5. Marking Requirements

5.1 Digital products must include clear and accessible marking indicating;

  1. the identity and version of the Product, and;

  2. the legal identity of the Developer, and;

  3. the legal identity of the Operator, and;

  4. for products of Class B or above, the address of permanent UK premises at which the Operator can be found.

C. Conformity & Assurance

Manufacturers must demonstrate and declare conformity before market entry.

For products that are intended for use by, or licensing to, over 1 million users, and that may reasonably be expected to be used by an individual for over 1 hour per week, manufacturers must also hold a valid certificate of approval from an Approved Body authorised by the UK Government.

1. Technical Documentation (Technical File)

1.1 The Developer and Operator shall create, maintain and retain a Technical File. The Technical File shall include the following components.

  1. Product Description

    a description of the product sufficient to identify its intended purpose, principal functions, intended users, operating environment, reasonably foreseeable conditions of use and misuse, and any significant limitations on its use or operation.

  2. Product Design & Source Code

    a description of the product's design, architecture and operation, including its principal components, systems, interfaces and other technical characteristics necessary to understand how the product functions, accompanied by at least 12 months history of the products master and release code repositories.

  3. Risk Assessment

    a systematic assessment identifying the hazards presented by the product under intended and reasonably foreseeable conditions of use, the persons or groups who may be affected, the mechanisms by which harm may arise, and the nature and magnitude of the resulting risks. The Assessment shall include a description of the design measures, protective measures, operational controls, safety features and user information implemented to eliminate hazards or reduce risks so far as reasonably practicable, together with an assessment of any residual risks.

  4. Demonstration of Conformity

    evidence that the product has been appropriately evaluated to demonstrate that the product performs safely and as intended under representative operating conditions in accordance with all applicable essential requirements, including the results of testing, inspection, simulation, analysis, user evaluation, engineering safety analyses and other methods relied upon to establish compliance.

  5. State of the Art Documentation

    a State of the Art Assessment, which must:

    1. identify relevant safety technologies, design patterns, and controls available at the time of assessment;

    2. reference applicable standards, guidance, and industry practices;

    3. evaluate the suitability of available measures for the product;

    4. document justification where such measures are not adopted.

  6. Post-Market Safety

    Information relating to the continued safety of the product after it has been placed on the market, including arrangements for monitoring product performance, recording and investigating safety incidents, maintaining State of the Art, assessing newly identified risks, implementing corrective actions, issuing safety updates, and withdrawing, recalling or otherwise mitigating unsafe products where necessary.

  7. Declaration of Conformity

    a formal statement issued by the Provider declaring that the product complies with all applicable legislative requirements and that the necessary conformity assessment procedures have been completed.

2. Risk Assessment

2.1 General

A risk assessment shall be carried out by the developer. For each specified hazard area, the developer shall identify all and any risks, including but not restricted to those resulting from architecture, functionality, design affordances or user interface characteristics.

The developer shall consider not only the intended normal use, but also reasonably foreseeable use and misuse of the digital product.

The developer shall identify, evaluate and mitigate risks throughout the entire lifecycle of the digital product and the functions constituting the digital product, including (but not limited to) the following stages: design, development, deployment, operation, and retirement.

2.2 Risk Classification

Risks shall be classified based on a total risk score;

Risk = Severity x Exposure x Vulnerability

Overall Risk Score Classification
S x E x V > 100 Class B
S x E x V > 175 Class C

2.3 Risk Mitigation

In selecting the most appropriate solutions, the developer shall apply the principles set out below, in the following order:

Preventative Mitigations

  1. eliminating unnecessary functions from system requirements;

  2. substituting features in the architecture of the system in relation to risks that cannot be eliminated;

    3. Note: If either of the above preventative mitigation principles are applied, the risk assessment may be re-scored.

Protective Mitigations

  1. implementing active functional controls in relation to risks that cannot be substituted;

    2. Note: If any risk is assessed as Class B (following re-scoring for any preventative mitigations implemented) then at least one protective mitigation must be implemented.

    If any risk is assessed as Class C, then at least two, independent and distinct protective mitigations must be implemented.

    When implementing active controls, the developer must:

    1. Identify available state-of-the-art mitigations relevant to that risk;

    2. Assess their effectiveness in reducing severity, exposure, or vulnerability;

    3. Determine whether implementation is:

      • technically feasible,

      • proportionate to the level of risk,

      • compatible with system architecture;

    4. Incorporate such mitigations unless justified otherwise.

      5. Where a risk remains classified as Class B or Class C, failure to implement available state-of-the-art mitigations shall require explicit justification.

Reactive Mitigations

  1. instructing and implementing operational and procedural controls in relation to residual risks that cannot be functionally controlled;

  2. as a measure of last resort, applying requirements, conditions of use, instructions or information to digital service users in relation to residual risks, only where other forms of risk mitigation are not possible.

    3. Note: Reactive Mitigations cannot be considered when scoring risks, and cannot contribute to the mitigations considered to satisfy the Class B and Class C requirements.

Mitigations may address hazard severity, exposure (for example by empowering the user to more easily reduce or regulate their contact with the product) or vulnerability (for example by actively detecting or classifying user vulnerabilities and adapting its functionality or parametrisation).

Third-Party (Approved Body) Conformity Assessment & Certification

A third-party conformity assessment shall comprise an independent and documented assessment by an Approved Body to determine whether a product satisfies the applicable Essential Requirements of this Regulation.

The assessment shall, as appropriate to the product and its risk classification, include:

  1. examination of the product's intended purpose, reasonably foreseeable use and associated hazards;

  2. review of the product's design, architecture and technical documentation, including the Technical File;

  3. verification that appropriate risk assessment, testing and validation have been undertaken;

  4. assessment of conformity with applicable designated standards, technical specifications or other recognised means of demonstrating compliance;

  5. evaluation of the adequacy of manufacturing, development, quality management or operational control processes necessary to ensure continuing conformity;

  6. assessment of the product's provisions for post-market monitoring, incident reporting and corrective action;

  7. identification of any non-conformities and the actions necessary to achieve compliance; and

  8. issuance of a certificate, report or other determination recording whether the product conforms to the requirements of this Regulation.

The Approved Body shall conduct its assessment impartially, independently and on the basis of sufficient technical evidence. Certification shall not relieve the Developer or any other economic operator of their obligations under this Regulation.

5. Declaration of Conformity

The Declaration of Conformity shall be prepared by the Developer before the product is placed on the market or put into service and shall include, as a minimum:

  1. the name, registered trade name or trademark, and contact details of the Developer and, where applicable, any authorised representative;

  2. sufficient information to uniquely identify the product, including its name, model, version, batch, serial number or other identifying reference;

  3. a statement that the Declaration is issued under the sole responsibility of the Developer;

  4. identification of the applicable legislation under which conformity is declared;

  5. a list of the designated standards, technical specifications or other conformity assessment methods applied;

  6. where applicable, details of any Approved Body involved in the conformity assessment, including the certificate or assessment reference;

  7. a declaration that the product conforms to the applicable requirements of this Regulation;

  8. the date and place of issue of the Declaration;

  9. the name, position and signature, or equivalent authenticated approval, of the person authorised to sign on behalf of the Developer.

The Declaration of Conformity shall be maintained as part of the Technical File, kept up to date where necessary, and made available to competent authorities upon request.

D. Economic Operator Obligations

0. Definitions

Product

A product is any software or digital content that is sold, licensed or made available for use in the UK, in the course of a commercial activity, whether for payment or free of charge, that is directly operable or capable of being sensed by a human user.

It includes software that has a human interface, or which directly determines the content or behaviour of a human interface.

It includes websites, apps, programs, or software-generated services where the user directly interacts with software without a human intermediary.

User

User includes any individual user and any collective user.

Individual users include the intended consumers, or anyone reasonably likely to use the product.

Collective users are understood at a societal level and include people who could reasonably expect to be impacted by the product even if they do not directly use it themselves.

1. Developer Obligations

Deﬁnition

Developer means any natural or legal person who:

  1. designs a system or software, owns product source code, creates non-user content or undertakes compilation of product binaries. This includes the commissioner, procurer or licensee of any components that are packaged into a final product or directly-user-accessible service.

  2. has a product designed or developed, or who, by putting their name, trademark or other distinguishing features on that product, presents themselves as its developer; or

  3. develops a product for their own use.

Obligations

The Developer shall:

  1. ensure that the product complies with the Essential Requirements throughout its lifecycle;

  2. undertake and document risk assessment, testing and conformity assessment;

  3. prepare, maintain and make available the Technical File and other prescribed technical documentation;

  4. ensure that all required certifications, registrations, licences, declarations and conformity markings are completed before the product is placed on the market or put into service;

  5. implement appropriate quality, security and change-management processes to ensure that software updates and modifications continue to satisfy the Essential Requirements;

  6. undertake post-market monitoring, investigate incidents and emerging hazards and implement timely corrective measures where necessary, including safety updates, feature restrictions, withdrawal, suspension or recall-equivalent measures; and

  7. undertake ongoing assessment of the State of the Art and implement relevant and safety features as they become commercially available; and

  8. cooperate with competent authorities, Approved Bodies, Operators and Distributors in demonstrating conformity and managing product safety throughout the product lifecycle.

2. Operator Obligations

Deﬁnition

The Operator includes any economic operator that runs a directly-user-accessible service, this includes content that is served or executed outside the UK but that allows access to users within the UK. It includes any economic operator who acts as a UK market gatekeeper in any way.

Obligations

The Operator shall:

  1. place on the UK market or make available only products that it has reasonable grounds to believe comply with this Regulation;

  2. verify, before placing a product on the market or putting it into service, that the required conformity assessment, technical documentation and declarations are available;

  3. maintain appropriate records to ensure the traceability of products placed on the market and the identity of the responsible Developer;

  4. ensure that operational deployment, configuration and management of the product do not compromise compliance with the Essential Requirements.

  5. implement proportionate operational safeguards, including policeability measures where required by this Regulation;

  6. monitor information relating to product safety within its control and promptly notify the Developer and competent authorities where significant risks are identified;

  7. Ensure that ongoing assessment of the state of the art are carried out and that identified developments are implemented in a timely manner;

  8. cooperate with competent authorities in the provision of information, records and technical assistance necessary for market surveillance and enforcement; and

  9. cooperate in the implementation of corrective measures, including safety updates, restrictions, withdrawal or recall-equivalent actions.

3. Distributor Obligations

Deﬁnition

The Distributor includes (but is not limited to) app stores, digital marketplaces, cloud service resellers and device vendors (including when bundling software).

Obligations

The Distributor shall:

  1. act with due care to ensure that only compliant digital products are made available on the market;

  2. verify that the product is accompanied by the required conformity information, user information and other prescribed documentation before making it available;

  3. refrain from supplying, promoting or continuing to distribute a product where it has reasonable grounds to believe that the product does not comply with this Regulation;

  4. cooperate with the Developer, Operator and competent authorities in the investigation of safety concerns and the implementation of corrective measures; and

  5. promptly cease distribution and assist in the implementation of safety updates, withdrawal, suspension or recall-equivalent measures where these are necessary to protect users.

E. Standards & Technical Codification

Government should convene with industry and civic society to identify a schedule of priority standards for development.

Government should run and commission accelerated programmes to develop working versions of these norms at pace, which can be iterated and matured until contents stabilises. Full standards may then be developed and subsequently designated under the proposed Digital Product Safety Regulations. Work towards international alignment and adoption would be carried out in parallel.

It is proposed that parts of the whole framework are developed in parallel, but with high-priority (high impact and/or urgency) norms being identified and developed at pace first, followed by subsequent waves of required new standards.

F. Institutions

1. Existing Institutions

Government

HM Government has the key leadership role in implementing a Product Safety regulation. It’s role is not only to introduce the regulation, but to practically lead the program of standards development, and to introduce new institutions. This will require close collaboration between the Department for Science, Innovation & Technology (DSIT), and the Department for Business & Trade (DBT).

Ofcom has a role in enforcing the legal constraints on digital providers. This role is not demoted by the introduction of Digital Product Safety. While Ofcom enforces the legal constraints on Providers imposed by the Online Safety Act (OSA) and conditions implemented under the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act, Digital Product Safety is focussed on establishing new expectations of safety for hazardous, but not explicitly prohibited, functions, capabilities and characteristics of digital products.
It is recommended that Ofcom could also administer Approved Body status for independent third-party certification bodies, and also operate the Digital Operator Registration & Licensing Scheme for products of Class D and E .

Standards Bodies

The British Standards Institution (BSI) is the UK’s National (and the wold’s first) Standards Body. It holds key convening power and processes for the development of new standards. BSI can develop informal standards at pace through the Flex process or as Publicly Available Specifications (PAS). It is recommended that BSI is engaged initially to assist in prioritising a programme of standards work, and then in delivering prioritised waves of digital product safety standards according to the priorities set by government, industry and other relevant experts and stakeholders.

CEN/CENELEC and ISO/IEC are international standards bodies at European and global level consecutively. They should also be engaged early on, to ensure that opportunities for alignment are maximised. Market divergence is inevitable, but international alignment, in particular with key trade partners, is extremely beneficial and the UK has a strong track record of setting norms that are adopted both in Europe and globally.

The AI Security Institute (AISI) is a research organisation within the Department of Science, Innovation and Technology. It has relevant expertise in the testing of digital systems for safety and security, and should be engaged in a process of accelerated standards development.

The IEEE is the world’s largest technical professional organisation. It has developed a range of technology standards relevant to digital safety, and should also be engaged in the rapid development of standards.

The National Physical Laboratory (NPL) is the UK's National Metrology Institute (NMI). It is a public sector research establishment, with high capabilities in the capture and analysis of data. NPL is a potential advisory partner in the development of standards for trust and confidence in AI systems and safe AI adoption.

2. New Institutions

Approved Bodies

Under Digital Product Safety, Class B or higher products must be certified by an independent third party. As with other product safety regulations, it is proposed that third parties be authorised for this work as Approved Bodies under the Regulation. Existing businesses could apply for this status, but the new role would be established by the Regulation.

Proposed Transitional Digital Product Safety Authority

The most important new institution is a proposed temporary Transitional Digital Product Safety Authority. This authority will have a range of powers of enforcement, including powers to impose recalls and commercial disincentives, similar to existing powers in the Office of Product Safety, Ofcom and ICO. Eventually, this authority may be disbanded, but it is proposed to operate until digital safety in UK markets has normalised to an equivalent level to existing product safety.

G. Enforcement

Transitional Digital Product Safety Authority

1 Deﬁnitions

1.1 "digital product" means any software, system, model, algorithm or artificial intelligence system, or any feature of such, whether or not embodied in or supplied together with tangible goods, that is made available to persons in the United Kingdom;

1.2 "online marketplace" and "online product" have the meanings given in the Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025 as applied to digital products by section 3 of this measure;

1.3 "ancillary service" means a payment service, advertising facilitation service (including an ad network or ad server), or search or discovery service that facilitates the provision of a digital product, whether provided from within or outside the United Kingdom;

1.4 "access facility" means an internet access service, app store, or other facility by which a UK user obtains or maintains access to a digital product;

1.5 "business disruption measure" means a service restriction order or an access restriction order made under Part 5, extending to digital products the category of measure provided for online safety services by Part 7, Chapter 6 of the Online Safety Act 2023;

1.6 "service restriction order" means a measure of Tier 1, 2 or 3 (Schedule 1) requiring a provider of an ancillary service to withdraw, adapt or restrict its service in relation to a specified digital product;

1.7 "access restriction order" means a measure of Tier 4 (Schedule 1) requiring a provider of an access facility to impede access to a specified digital product by persons in the United Kingdom;

1.8 "the Authority" means the Transitional Digital Product Safety Authority established by section 4;

1.9 "the Tribunal" means the tribunal or tribunal chamber designated under section 7.

2 Transitional Digital Product Safety Authority

2.1 Extension of product-safety concepts to digital products.

The product and market-surveillance concepts in the Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025 apply to digital products as they apply to tangible products, subject to the modifications in Schedule 2.

2.2 Establishment.

There is established a body corporate to be known as the Transitional Digital Product Safety Authority, operating within the Office for Product Safety and Standards structure, with functions under this Act and under the Product Regulation and Metrology Act 2025 so far as relating to digital products.

2.3 Objectives.

The Authority must exercise its functions so as to protect users of digital products from harm, secure effective and proportionate enforcement, and support innovation and lawful competition; and must have regard, in exercising any function, to the safeguards in Part 6.

2.4 Concurrent exercise of functions; statutory cooperation.

The Authority's functions under this measure are exercisable concurrently with the functions of the Office of Communications, the Information Commissioner, the Competition and Markets Authority and the Financial Conduct Authority under their respective enactments. The Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum is placed on a statutory footing as the coordinating body for the exercise of concurrent functions under this measure, and must publish a memorandum of understanding governing case allocation.

2.5 Designation of Tribunal.

The Secretary of State must by regulations designate a tribunal or tribunal chamber (which may be an extension of the jurisdiction of the Competition Appeal Tribunal, or a new chamber of the General Regulatory Chamber) to exercise the functions of the Tribunal under this measure, including a duty to determine expedited appeals under section 28 within 5 working days so far as reasonably practicable. The Tribunal route is adopted in preference to the High Court application required for a business disruption measure under section 144 of the Online Safety Act 2023, so as to enable the faster, operationally-toggled model of authorisation set out in Part 5.

3 General Powers

3.1 Information and document notices.

The Authority may, by notice, require a person to provide information or produce documents reasonably required for the exercise of its functions, including a requirement to commission and pay for a technical report by an approved independent person.

3.2 Entry, inspection, testing and audit.

An authorised officer may, with a warrant where the premises are a dwelling, enter premises to inspect, test (including adversarial or "red-team" testing of a digital product), and require the production of records; and may seize items reasonably required as evidence.

3.3 Incident reporting.

A provider of a digital product designated under regulations as higher-risk must notify the Authority and the National Cyber Security Centre of a qualifying incident within 24 hours of becoming aware of it, and provide a full report within 72 hours.

3.4 Risk assessment duty.

A provider of a designated higher-risk digital product must carry out and keep under review a risk assessment in a form specified by the Authority, and must provide it on request.

3.5 Technical audit and public advisory notice.

The Authority may publish the results of testing or inspection carried out under section 9, together with a public advisory recommending that persons in the United Kingdom cease using, or refrain from acquiring, a specified digital product, where it reasonably considers this necessary to protect users from a risk identified by that testing or inspection. A notice under this section is not a business disruption measure for the purposes of Part 5 and does not require a framework order; the Authority may withdraw it at any time.

4 Recall and Corrective Action Powers

4.1 Compliance notice.

The Authority may require a person to take specified steps within a specified period to remedy a failure to comply with a product requirement applicable to a digital product.

4.2 Stop notice.

The Authority may require a person to cease making a digital product, or a specified feature of it, available to persons in the United Kingdom, where it presents a risk.

4.3 Feature-recall notice.

The Authority may require a person to withdraw, disable, or roll back a digital product or feature to a specified prior compliant state, and to notify affected users, within a specified period.

4.4 Forfeiture and deletion.

On application by the Authority, a court may order the forfeiture, disposal, or deletion of a non-compliant digital product, dataset, or model, where a lesser remedy would not adequately address the risk.

4.5 Monetary penalties.

The Authority may impose a penalty not exceeding the greater of £18 million or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue for a breach of a notice under this Part, aligned with the penalty ceilings in the Online Safety Act 2023 and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025; and a further penalty not exceeding £20,000 per day for continuing non-compliance, aligned with the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2022.

5 Business Disruption Measures

5.1 Framework orders.

The Authority may apply to the Tribunal for a framework order in respect of a named digital product where it considers that a failure to comply with an enforceable requirement is causing, or is likely to cause, significant harm to users in the United Kingdom, and that a compliance notice, stop notice or feature-recall notice under Part 4 has been tried and proved insufficient, or would be likely to prove insufficient. A framework order authorises the Authority to make service restriction orders, and, only as provided in section 20, access restriction orders, against the named digital product, and must specify — (a) the tier or tiers of measure authorised, by reference to Schedule 1; (b) objective criteria that must be met before the Authority may make, vary or withdraw an order under it; (c) the maximum severity and duration of any single order; and (d) an expiry date not more than 90 days after the framework order is made, which the Tribunal may renew on application supported by fresh evidence. This section extends to digital products the business disruption jurisdiction provided for online safety services by section 144 of the Online Safety Act 2023.

5.2 Service restriction orders.

Under a framework order, the Authority may make a service restriction order requiring a provider of an ancillary service to withdraw, adapt or restrict its service in relation to the named digital product, scoped to Tier 1, 2 or 3 (Schedule 1).

5.3 Access restriction orders.

The Authority may make an access restriction order requiring a provider of an access facility to impede access to the named digital product by persons in the United Kingdom, scoped to Tier 4 (Schedule 1), only where a service restriction order made under the same framework order has been tried and found insufficient, or would clearly be insufficient given the nature of the harm — mirroring the sequencing of sections 144 to 146 of the Online Safety Act 2023, under which Ofcom may proceed to an access restriction order only once a service restriction order is shown, or would be shown, not to suffice.

5.4 Operational toggles.

Once a framework order is in force, the Authority may make, vary or withdraw a service restriction order or access restriction order within its terms without further application to the Tribunal, provided that each order is — (a) logged in a register maintained by the Authority; (b) notified to the affected person and, so far as consistent with operational security, published; and (c) withdrawn automatically upon the Authority being satisfied that the specified compliance criteria have been met.

5.5 Graduated application.

The Authority must apply the lowest tier reasonably likely to be effective, and may only make an access restriction order where it is satisfied that Tiers 1 to 3 have been tried and found insufficient, or would clearly be insufficient given the nature of the harm.

5.6 Relationship to net neutrality and other enactments.

An access restriction order is to be treated as a measure necessary to comply with a legal requirement for the purposes of the retained Regulation (EU) 2015/2120 and any successor instrument governing open internet access. Nothing in this Part authorises a measure that could not lawfully be ordered by a court under section 97A of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 or under Part 7, Chapter 6 of the Online Safety Act 2023, save as expressly provided.

5.7 Third-party addressees.

A service restriction order or access restriction order may impose requirements only on a provider of an ancillary service or access facility, and must be proportionate to the smallest scope of that provider's service reasonably capable of addressing the harm (for example, a specified payment flow or advertising placement, rather than the whole of the provider's relationship with the non-compliant service).

6 Safeguards, Rights and Oversight

6.1 Proportionality and necessity duty.

The Authority must not exercise a power under Part 4 or Part 5 unless it is satisfied that doing so is a necessary and proportionate response to the risk identified, having considered less restrictive alternatives.

6.2 Human rights and property compliance.

The Authority must exercise its functions compatibly with the rights protected by Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights and Article 1 of the First Protocol to that Convention, and must publish guidance on how it interprets this duty in the business-disruption context.

6.3 Publication of criteria.

The Authority must publish, and keep under review, the objective criteria it will apply in deciding whether to seek a framework order and whether to make, vary, or withdraw a service restriction order or access restriction order under it, before any such criteria are relied upon.

6.4 Rights of representation and appeal.

A person affected by a notice under Part 4, or by the making or variation of a service restriction order or access restriction order under Part 5, may make representations to the Authority and may appeal to the Tribunal; an appeal against a Tier 3 order or an access restriction order is an expedited appeal for the purposes of section 7, to be determined within 5 working days so far as reasonably practicable.

6.5 Transparency reporting.

The Authority must publish an annual report setting out the number and nature of notices, service restriction orders and access restriction orders used under Parts 4 and 5, their duration, the outcome of any appeals, and its assessment of their effectiveness and proportionality, and must lay this report before Parliament.

7 Review and Sunset

7.1 Statutory review.

The Secretary of State must, before the end of the period of 5 years beginning with the date this measure comes into force, complete and lay before Parliament a review of the operation of this measure, including whether the Authority's functions should be continued, modified, transferred to another body, or allowed to lapse.

7.2 Sunset.

Unless Parliament resolves to continue it, this measure, and the Authority's functions under it, expire at the end of the period of 6 years beginning with the date this measure comes into force.

Schedule 1 — Graduated Tiers of Business Disruption Measure

  • Tier 1: visibility/distribution measures (interstitial warnings; de-ranking; removal from recommender or featured placement) — made by service restriction order (section 19).

  • Tier 2: advertising-facilitation suspension, scoped to the specific non-compliant product or feature — made by service restriction order (section 19).

  • Tier 3: payment or in-app purchase flow suspension, scoped to the specific non-compliant product or feature — made by service restriction order (section 19).

  • Tier 4: time-boxed deprioritisation (not blocking) of a narrowly defined traffic class by an access facility — made by access restriction order (section 20), available only as a reserve power.
